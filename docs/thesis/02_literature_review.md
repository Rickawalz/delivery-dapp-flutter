## 2.1 Introduction

Chapter 1 introduced the problem of keeping track of records and the main argument of this thesis: a blockchain logistics platform can start under centralized administration and move toward full decentralization through rules written into smart contracts. This chapter reviews the research and technical ideas behind that argument. It starts with visibility and traceability. Those topics sit at the center of the project, because information about a single shipment is often spread across databases, documents, messages, and physical scans that belong to different organizations, and prior studies explain that this separation can make it harder for organizations to share information and trust the records they receive [11], [1].

The chapter also explains blockchain, smart contracts, decentralized applications (DApps), and digital wallets, the technologies that let RLE record delivery events and let users interact with the system. Blockchain has an important limitation, though. It can protect information after it is submitted. It cannot prove that a physical package is actually where a person or device says it is, and connecting a real shipment to its digital record remains one of the main problems in blockchain supply chain systems [4], [12].

Later sections discuss off-chain storage, privacy, tokens, governance, and progressive decentralization. These topics matter because RLE is not meant to be fully decentralized on the first day: the prototype starts with an administrator who manages important functions, and control is designed to move from that administrator to the people who use the system as the network reaches milestones written into a smart contract. The chapter also reviews the code-level mechanisms that existing blockchain projects already use to limit administrative control. It ends with a comparison. RLE is set beside other blockchain supply chain research, and the research gap it is designed to address is explained.

The sections of this chapter map onto the research questions presented in Chapter 1. Sections 2.2 and 2.3 provide the background for RQ1, which concerns transparent and auditable shipment records, while Sections 2.4 and 2.5 support RQ2 by explaining how smart contracts, wallets, applications, and off-chain data fit together. Sections 2.7 and 2.8, on token incentives, governance, and progressive decentralization, support RQ3. Section 2.6, together with the limitations discussed throughout the chapter, frames the evaluation called for by RQ4.

## 2.2 Supply Chain Visibility and Traceability

Supply chain visibility means being able to see useful and timely information about goods, orders, and events as they move through a supply chain. Traceability is related. It looks back at the history of a product or shipment, so that visibility tells users what is happening now while traceability tells them what happened earlier. For a delivery, that history may include when it was created, who accepted it, which checkpoints were recorded, whether pickup and delivery were confirmed, and when the delivery was completed.

Chapter 1 described how a warehouse, a carrier, and a customer can each hold a different record of the same delivery. None of those records has to be completely wrong for confusion to occur; the bigger problem is that the groups do not share one reliable history. Chang, Iakovou, and Shi describe global supply chains as networks made up of many parties, documents, standards, and information systems [1]. Dutta et al. agree. They identify visibility, tracking, coordination, and trust as major reasons for studying blockchain in supply chain operations [11].

Sharing information does not automatically create trust. Whoever runs a shared platform may control access, decide how mistakes are corrected, and choose what information users can see. That is not always a problem. A centralized database can be fast, affordable, and easy to manage, and it may be the best choice when one trusted organization owns the process. Independent organizations, however, may not want one company to control the entire history, especially when they have no way to know whether a record was added late, changed, or deleted.

Several organizations sharing information is not, by itself, a reason to use blockchain. Reviews by Dutta et al. and Queiroz et al. suggest that blockchain makes the most sense when different organizations update the same records, do not completely trust one another, and need a shared audit trail [11], [13]. When everyone already accepts one organization as the system owner, a regular database is usually simpler and less expensive. RLE is built on this distinction. The project examines where a shared ledger adds value and where central control remains the practical choice.

A ledger is only as useful as the information placed in it. Blockchain can make a confirmed entry difficult to change, but it cannot stop a driver, customer, sensor, or administrator from submitting inaccurate information, and this point appears again and again in recent research. Reviews identify problems with linking products to digital records, creating common standards, handling large transaction volumes, connecting different systems, and gaining support from participants [4], [12], [14]. Traceability therefore needs more than a secure ledger. It also depends on responsible users, clear access rules, and dependable ways to capture real-world events.

## 2.3 Blockchain Technology

Chapter 1 introduced blockchain as a shared digital ledger in which transactions are grouped together, linked with cryptography, and copied across a network [2]. This section looks more closely at the parts of that design that matter for RLE. Current supply chain research describes blockchain as a shared record that makes confirmed changes easier to detect [11], [14]. Its value still depends on three things: how the network is designed, who controls it, and whether the information entered into it is accurate.

A hash works like a digital fingerprint. A hash function changes data into a fixed-length value, and even a small change in the original data produces a different result, which is why blocks use these values to connect to earlier blocks and reveal later changes. RLE uses the same idea for private files. Instead of placing a full file on-chain, it can store the file's hash; when the file is checked later, the system creates the hash again and compares it with the saved value. A match shows that the file has not changed. It does not show that the original file was accurate. Hashing should also not be confused with encryption.

The type of blockchain matters too. Public networks allow broad participation, while permissioned networks limit access to approved organizations. Both depend on consensus, the process that allows network computers to agree on the accepted ledger, and the chosen method affects security, speed, energy use, and participation. Supply chain projects often consider permissioned networks because the companies are already known and some business information is sensitive [11], [1], [13]. Public networks reduce dependence on a single organization. They may also bring changing fees, visible transaction data, and less predictable confirmation times.

Blockchain records are often called immutable, meaning they cannot be changed. Tamper-resistant is more accurate. Confirmed records are difficult to change because an attacker would have to overcome the network rules and alter connected data, yet blockchain systems can still face software bugs, stolen keys, attacks, contract upgrades, and governance decisions. Recent reviews explain that security depends on the design and management of the whole system, not only on the blockchain [4], [14]. RLE therefore treats blockchain as a stronger audit record rather than a perfect guarantee.

These advantages come with practical costs. Ethereum transactions require computing resources and gas fees, and the network stores copies of information across many computers, which is less efficient than saving the information once in a normal database. Privacy is another concern. Transactions may remain visible for a long time, and studies published since 2020 continue to report problems involving speed, compatibility, regulation, privacy, integration, and adoption [11], [1], [4], [12], [13], [14], [15]. RLE does not try to place everything on Ethereum. It stores selected events and proof hashes on-chain and leaves other information in regular application services.

## 2.4 Ethereum, Smart Contracts, Wallets, and DApps

Ethereum expanded blockchain by allowing developers to create smart contracts and DApps. The Ethereum White Paper and Yellow Paper describe a blockchain that can store information and run programs [16], [17], and Christidis and Devetsikiotis show how the same idea can automate agreements and processes between parties that do not share one system [3]. A smart contract is a program placed at a blockchain address. Users or other contracts call its functions through transactions, and the network then follows the rules written in the contract. Smart contracts can automate many tasks, but mistakes in the code can affect the entire system. Research has identified common security problems involving access control, contract states, arithmetic operations, and interactions between contracts [18].

A delivery contract shows what smart contracts can do. It can prevent a checkpoint from being recorded before the delivery exists, or restrict completion to an approved user, and its events create a history that the application can display. Rules like these reduce the need for an administrator to check every action by hand. The drawback is simple: the contract follows its code even when the code is wrong. One small error in authorization or in the order of delivery states can affect every user, so testing, limited administrative access, and a careful upgrade plan are necessary [18].

A digital wallet allows a user to manage a blockchain account and approve transactions. It protects the private keys used to create digital signatures, and the network checks each signature to confirm that the correct account approved the transaction. A wallet does not normally store the application's information. It provides identity and permission at the blockchain level. Wallets also create risks. A user may lose a key, reveal a recovery phrase, approve a harmful transaction, or choose the wrong network, and these tasks can be confusing for people who have little experience with blockchain.

A DApp combines blockchain contracts with an application that people can use. The word decentralized may suggest that every part of the application is decentralized. Often it is not. Many DApps use a mixed design in which smart contracts store shared rules and transaction history while regular servers handle searches, images, notifications, reports, and user profiles. This design can improve speed and make the application easier to use, but it also leaves some parts of the system under the control of the application owner.

RLE uses this mixed design. Ethereum smart contracts manage the delivery process, user roles, proof hashes, fees, rewards, and governance information; the Flutter application gives customers, drivers, and administrators a visual interface; and wallet services approve blockchain transactions. Firebase and Firestore hold application information that would be too expensive, too private, or too inefficient to place directly on the blockchain. Chapter 3 explains how these parts were built. In its current form, RLE still relies on an administrator and cloud services, so it is not yet fully decentralized. Moving administrative control to participants is the job of the milestone-based contract described in Section 2.8. The cloud services are a separate issue, and Chapter 4 discusses how much the system still depends on them.

## 2.5 Connecting Physical Shipments to Blockchain Records

Consider a package that is scanned as loaded even though it is still sitting in the warehouse. A blockchain can preserve the scan and show which account submitted it. It cannot look at the loading area and correct the event. This is one of the hardest problems in blockchain tracking, and recent reviews agree that a shared ledger still depends on people, labels, sensors, and organizations for its original information [4], [12], [14]. If the input is wrong, the blockchain may create a strong record of the wrong event.

The oracle problem is another version of the same issue. An oracle is a person, device, or service that provides outside information to a smart contract, and if it reports the wrong temperature or location, the contract may accept that value and use it exactly as programmed. Caldarelli explains that different oracle designs change where trust is placed, but none removes trust completely [19]. Botta et al. make a related argument about digital twins. A useful tracking system, in their view, must maintain a believable connection between the physical product and its digital representation [20].

Supply chain systems use different methods to make this connection stronger, and each has a weakness. Barcodes and QR codes are inexpensive but can be copied. RFID tags can be scanned automatically, but they require special readers and can be removed. Phones and GPS devices report locations that may be late, inaccurate, or changed, while sensors that record temperature, movement, and location bring their own security, maintenance, and internet-connection problems. A system may also ask two or more participants to confirm an event or compare information from different sources.

Hashing can help protect information without placing private details on the blockchain. An application can store the hash of a location record or photograph instead of the full file, keep the original in protected off-chain storage, and later hash the file again to compare the result with the on-chain value. That comparison shows whether the file changed. Hashing alone may not protect simple information that an attacker can guess, however. Encryption, access controls, salts, and secure storage may still be needed.

RLE stores hashed checkpoints and proof information to demonstrate this approach. The system can show that a submitted record has not changed since its hash was added to the blockchain. It cannot prove that the location was real, that a photograph showed the correct package, or that the user was actually present. These limits mark what the prototype can and cannot demonstrate. The guessability concern applies directly here. Chapter 1 lists the protection of exact location information as one of the study's objectives, and a hash of raw coordinates could be reversed by an attacker who tests likely locations, so whether RLE's checkpoint hashes protect location privacy depends on how they are constructed. Chapter 3 describes that construction; Chapter 4 evaluates the protection it provides. Future versions could use approved devices, signed sensor information, confirmation from several users, or stronger connections between physical items and digital identities.

## 2.6 Privacy, Security, Performance, and Usability

A permanent public record creates an uncomfortable tradeoff for supply chains. Organizations may want a shared delivery history, but they also need to protect addresses, exact locations, prices, photographs, and business relationships, and placing this information directly on a public blockchain could expose it for a long time. Wallet addresses do not solve the problem. Transaction patterns may still reveal who is involved. Han and Park show that blockchain features such as permanent records and distributed responsibility can conflict with data-protection rules, including the right to erase personal information [21].

Security problems can also exist outside the blockchain. Attackers may steal private keys, compromise the application, or change an outside database; administrators may misuse their access; and smart contracts may contain coding mistakes, including weak authorization or unsafe state changes [18]. A secure blockchain cannot fix an insecure mobile application. Nor can it fix careless user behavior. Because RLE is a mixed system, its security depends on the contracts, wallets, Flutter application, cloud services, and administrators together.

Speed and cost lead to a different tradeoff. A regular database can normally process an update without waiting for a distributed network, whereas blockchain transactions may take time to confirm and may each require a fee. Large files are especially expensive to store on-chain. Dutta et al. discuss the possible operational value of blockchain, while later reviews still recommend choosing limited use cases with a clear reason for using a shared ledger [11], [4], [12]. RLE follows that advice. It records a small delivery history and proof hashes on-chain instead of storing photographs, documents, or continuous GPS data there.

Usability affects whether people will use the system at all. Most users understand a delivery status. Wallet addresses, gas fees, transaction signatures, and network errors are another matter, and too many approval screens or unclear error messages may send users back to familiar applications. The Flutter interface in RLE is designed to hide some of this complexity, but users must still approve wallet transactions, so a real deployment would require instructions, testing, and support.

## 2.7 Token-Based Participation and Incentives

Tokens are sometimes presented as an easy way to motivate participation. Their effect, though, depends on the rules around them. A token may represent money, access, voting power, or a reward, and NIST explains that a token system needs rules for creation, transfer, accounts, and governance [22]. Those technical rules are only part of the design. The developer must also decide why users earn tokens, what the tokens can be used for, how supply is controlled, and how the system will respond when users find ways to exploit the reward formula.

A logistics platform could reward drivers for accepting deliveries, reporting events on time, maintaining good ratings, or serving difficult routes, and a smart contract can make the reward formula visible and apply it consistently. Users may change their behavior to earn more. A reward based on delivery count might encourage someone to divide one shipment into several smaller deliveries, and a reward based on distance could encourage inefficient routes or false reports. Ratings may also be affected by retaliation, cooperation between dishonest users, or fake accounts.

Fake or multiple identities are a special concern in open systems. Douceur's foundational paper describes a Sybil attack, in which one person creates several identities to gain more influence [23]. Identity checks, deposits, reputation scores, rate limits, and approved enrollment may reduce this risk, but each of these solutions also makes the system less open. Token ownership creates a further problem. Early users or wealthy participants may gain too much voting power.

The RLE reward formula considers delivery volume, distance, ratings, and delivery diversity, with limits included to reduce simple forms of abuse. Even so, the prototype does not prove that the formula is fair or safe from coordinated attacks. Simulations can show unusual reward patterns and obvious weaknesses. Real behavior would require many users and repeated testing over time. RLE therefore treats its rewards as an experimental feature that can be measured and improved, not as a finished logistics economy.

The reward design also matters for governance. Chapter 1 describes token rewards as one of the tools RLE uses to move control toward participants, and if tokens earned through deliveries also determine influence over the platform, then every weakness in the reward formula becomes a weakness in governance as well. Consider a participant who inflates rewards through split deliveries, false distance reports, or Sybil identities. That person would gain more tokens and, after the milestones are reached, more control. Early participants could likewise build a lasting share of influence simply by joining first. The fairness of the reward formula and the distribution of control discussed in the next section should therefore be evaluated together rather than as separate questions.

## 2.8 Governance and Progressive Decentralization

Even a decentralized platform needs someone to decide how changes are made. Governance covers those decisions: how disputes are handled, how rules are updated, how emergencies are managed, and how shared resources are controlled. Recent research on DAOs looks at how voting works, who participates in decisions, and how organizations remain accountable when some of their rules are managed through code [24]–[26]. None of them treats governance as a problem that blockchain automatically solves. Smart contracts may enforce some choices, but developers, administrators, companies, laws, and community discussions still shape the system.

On-chain governance uses blockchain transactions for proposals, voting, and approved changes. Off-chain governance relies on meetings, online discussions, company policies, developer decisions, and legal agreements. Token voting makes votes easy to count, but people with many tokens may gain too much influence, and low participation can allow a small group to control the result. A voting contract can therefore be technically decentralized while real decision-making stays concentrated.

A decentralized autonomous organization, or DAO, is a blockchain-based group that uses smart contracts for some of its rules and decisions [24], [25]. Recent research describes DAOs as developing organizations rather than only computer programs [24]–[26]. A DAO can use proposals, voting, and shared funds without a traditional management structure. It still faces questions about legal responsibility, voter knowledge, emergency action, software errors, and unequal power. In logistics these concerns are sharper, because deliveries and customer problems may require quick decisions.

Progressive decentralization means that control is handed over in stages instead of all at once. Walden describes it as a practical strategy for crypto applications: a founding team keeps control early on so it can build the product, fix problems, and find users, and then gradually turns control over to the community [9]. The weak point is that the founding team still decides when, and whether, that handoff happens. Naef, Wagner, and Saur look at a similar tension in blockchain supply chain networks. They describe the balance between central leadership and decentralized control as a governance challenge, but they treat it as an organizational issue for the participants to work out, not as something the system itself enforces [6].

That leaves two problems. The first is motivation: once a platform has users, revenue, and an established team, the people in charge have little reason to give up control. The second is concentration. Even when control is formally handed over, it may not spread very far, as Messias et al. found when they studied governance voting in Compound and Uniswap and showed that in each system only ten voters held most of the voting power [10]. Barbereau et al. reached a similar conclusion across nine major decentralized finance projects, where tokenized voting rights were highly concentrated and rarely exercised [29]. Control handed over on paper is not control decentralized in practice.

Code can address part of the motivation problem. Existing blockchain projects already use several mechanisms to limit administrative control. The simplest is a timelock, which delays the execution of an approved change for a fixed period so that users can see the change before it takes effect and leave the system if they disagree [27]. Governor contracts go further. They move decisions such as upgrades and parameter changes to on-chain proposals and token-weighted votes, usually executed through a timelock [27]. Developers may also renounce an administrative role after deployment, so that the authority sits with a timelock or governance contract instead of the original deployer [27].

The closest parallel to RLE's staged approach comes from Ethereum rollups. Building on a proposal by Vitalik Buterin, L2BEAT introduced its Stages Framework in June 2023 to rate rollups from Stage 0, where a small number of operators control the system, to Stage 2, where control rests mainly with code and users have a long window to exit unwanted upgrades [28]. The idea is not new, then. Parts of the blockchain community already treat decentralization as a series of measurable stages rather than a single event.

These mechanisms differ from RLE in one important way. Timelocks, governor contracts, and role renunciation limit how power is used or transferred, but the decision to adopt them, and the timing of any handover, still rests with the team in control. The Stages Framework is an outside assessment. It rates projects against stated criteria but cannot force a project to advance. In each case, the motivation problem remains. RLE instead ties the handover to participation milestones written into its contract, so the transfer is triggered by measured network growth rather than by a later decision of the administrators. Table 2.1 includes these mechanisms for comparison.

RLE responds to the first problem by writing the transition into a smart contract. When the network reaches defined milestones, control moves from the original administrators to the participants, and the handoff depends on rules in the code rather than a later decision by the founders. A credible plan needs more than that. It also needs a clear list of the powers that will be transferred, limits on what administrators can do in the meantime, and open upgrade procedures. The second problem is harder. RLE does not assume that this design solves it, since control could still end up with a small number of participants after the milestones are reached, as it did in the systems Messias et al. studied. The prototype is not a complete DAO, and the milestones do not guarantee fair governance. What RLE does allow is a closer look at whether a code-enforced transition can create a believable path from founder control to full decentralization, and at which risks remain.

What full decentralization means in this thesis should be stated precisely. The milestone contract concerns control over RLE's smart contracts: administrative roles, the authority to change platform parameters, and the governance functions recorded on-chain. It does not by itself decentralize the off-chain parts of the system. The Firebase and Firestore services described in Section 2.4 remain under the operator's control in the prototype. Full decentralization therefore refers to the point at which no single administrator keeps special authority over the contract layer. Chapter 1 calls the party that holds this authority before the milestones are reached the founder, the original developers, or the administrators; all three terms describe the same party. Chapter 3 defines the specific milestones and the powers transferred at each one, and Chapter 4 examines how much the system still depends on centralized services.

## 2.9 Related Blockchain-Based Supply Chain Systems

Recent reviews describe a field that has grown well beyond early examples such as agri-food traceability [7] and blockchain integration with the Internet of Things [8]. Blockchain supply chain research now covers medicine, manufacturing, logistics, retail, and international trade [4], [12], [13], [14], [15]. The reviews differ in focus. Paliwal, Chandra, and Sharma organize the literature around sustainability and supply chain management [4], Dasaklis et al. look more closely at how traceability systems are implemented [12], and Karaduman and Gülhas give additional attention to security and data integrity [14]. Yet they reach a similar warning: blockchain can protect a record after submission, but it cannot guarantee that the original label, scan, inspection, or human report was correct.

Some recent studies try to create stronger connections between physical items and blockchain records. Botta et al. propose digital twins that can be verified, improving the link between a real product and its digital version [20], and Caldarelli reviews oracle methods that allow smart contracts to use information from outside the blockchain [19]. These ideas improve basic recordkeeping. They still depend on secure devices, correct enrollment, system monitoring, and trusted rules.

Public blockchain networks are not the only model used in business. Many supply chain systems are permissioned, meaning that only approved organizations may join and operate the network, which can protect business information and make cooperation easier. The tradeoff is control. Approved members may keep most of it. Chang, Iakovou, and Shi focus on challenges in global and cross-border supply chains [1], while Khan, Kumar, and Sahu review blockchain applications across the wider supply chain field [15]. Together with other recent reviews, their work shows that adoption depends on shared standards, partner support, regulation, compatibility, cost, and clear business value [11], [13], [14]. Technology alone does not drive adoption, Francisco and Swanson add; organizations also weigh the expected benefits, the influence of their partners, and how much they trust the system [5].

The studies reviewed in this chapter are useful, but they do not provide one settled answer. They show that blockchain can record product history, custody changes, payments, and business rules. Many, however, are based on concepts, a single industry, or small prototypes, and researchers use different testing methods, which makes direct comparison difficult. Governance and rewards often receive less attention than traceability [4], [12], [14], [15]. When governance is discussed, it is usually treated as something the participating organizations agree on, not as a rule written into the system itself [6]. ## 2.10 Synthesis, Research Gap, and Chapter Summary

Taken together, the literature gives qualified support for blockchain in supply chains. A shared ledger can improve the audit history when independent groups need to add information and do not want one organization to control every record. Smart contracts can manage delivery steps and permissions; DApps and wallets give users access to those functions; and hashes can protect the integrity of off-chain evidence without publishing complete files or exact locations. Tokens and governance contracts can place reward and voting rules into code. None of this is automatically useful. Each feature must solve a real problem in the delivery process.

The same literature also sets clear limits. Blockchain does not make physical information truthful, private, inexpensive, easy to use, or decentralized simply because it is stored on a ledger. People and devices still produce the original data. Public transactions bring privacy concerns and fees, contracts can contain mistakes, wallets can confuse users, and administrators may keep more control than expected, while tokens that encourage participation can also encourage cheating or concentrate power. These weaknesses are part of the system being evaluated. They are not minor details to be ignored.

The main research gap appears when these concerns are considered together. Prior work gives strong support for blockchain traceability [11], [13], [12], and newer reviews add security and data-integrity concerns [14], [15]. The move from central control to decentralization, however, is usually treated as an organizational problem [6], as a promise that founders choose whether to keep [9], or as a matter for voluntary code-level mechanisms and outside assessments [27], [28]. Evidence from systems that did hand over control shows that power can stay concentrated anyway [10], [29]. Whether a handover triggered by participation milestones can work in practice has rarely been tested. There is also a second, more practical gap. Few studies bring smart contracts, wallet transactions, hashed proofs, cloud services, user roles, rewards, and a decentralization mechanism together in one application that people can actually use. RLE addresses both gaps through a controlled proof of concept. It is not presented as ready for production. The goal is to test what works, what transactions cost, how rewards behave, whether the milestone contract can move control away from the original administrators, and where central control still remains.

This chapter reviewed the main research and technical ideas behind RLE, from supply chain visibility, blockchain, smart contracts, DApps, and wallets to physical tracking, privacy, tokens, and governance. The literature shows that blockchain is most useful when independent groups need a shared record and do not completely trust one organization to control it. Blockchain cannot verify physical events by itself. Decentralization, finally, depends on the system's design and governance, not simply on whether it uses blockchain. The chapter also reviewed the code-level mechanisms that existing projects use to limit administrative control and explained how RLE's milestone-based approach differs from them. Chapter 3 explains the research method and how RLE was designed and built.
