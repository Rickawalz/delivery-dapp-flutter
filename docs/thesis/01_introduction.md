# Reliable Logistics Exchange (RLE): A Blockchain-Based Delivery Tracking System with Progressive Decentralization

# CHAPTER 1

# INTRODUCTION

Supply chains connect the people and organizations involved in producing, storing, transporting, and delivering goods. A product may pass through a manufacturer, warehouse, transportation provider, distribution center, local carrier, and customer before its journey is complete. Each participant is most likely to use a different information system. When records are separated across these systems, it can become difficult to determine where a shipment is, who handled it, and whether an important event actually occurred [1]. These problems can lead to delays, disputes, duplicated work, and reduced trust among supply chain participants.

Blockchain technology offers a different way to record and share information. Instead of relying only on one organization to maintain a central database, a blockchain keeps a shared ledger whose entries are difficult to alter after they have been confirmed [2]. Smart contracts add programmable rules that can record events, control permissions, and carry out selected actions automatically [3]. For these reasons, the blockchain has drawn growing research interest as a tool for improving supply chain transparency, traceability and accountability [1], [4], [5].

This thesis looks at this idea by designing, building, and evaluating the Reliable Logistics Exchange (RLE), which is a blockchain-based program for tracking deliveries. RLE uses Ethereum smart contracts, a Flutter application, wallet integration, and Firebase/Firestore to support information that is stored outside of the blockchain. The system keeps track of deliveries, assigned participants, hashed location checkpoints, proof of pickup and delivery, completion details, ratings, platform fees, and token rewards.

The long-term goal of RLE is to become fully decentralized so no single organization, such as a manufacturer, carrier or platform operator, has complete control over the delivery records. This is important in supply chains because many different people and organizations need to rely on the same information, even when they may not fully trust each other. Customers, drivers, and other participants all depend on accurate delivery records, which makes decentralization useful in this type of system. However, making RLE fully decentralized from the beginning would be difficult. A new network needs people who are willing to participate, review the code, help secure the system, and maintain the infrastructure. These people will also expect to be compensated for their work. Because of this, some centralized control at the beginning can provide the funding and organization needed to get the system started.

Many blockchain projects say that they plan to become more decentralized over time, but this does not always happen. Once a project has users, money, and an established team, the people in control have little reason to give up that control, especially if decentralization is completely their decision [6]. RLE tries to address this problem by using a milestone-based smart contract instead of relying on a promise from the original developers. As the network reaches certain milestones, control of the system is gradually transferred from the original administrators to the participants. This would move more control of the delivery system toward the customers, drivers and other people who create and use the delivery records.

Because these milestones are written into the smart contract, the transfer of control is based on rules in the code rather than a future decision by one person or organization. However, there are still important questions that need to be considered. It is not known whether enough people will participate for the network to reach these milestones. It is also possible that the reward system may not be completely fair or that some users could find ways to take advantage of it. This thesis studies these problems instead of assuming that the system will automatically work as intended.

## 1.1 Background

Supply chain visibility is the ability to obtain useful information about goods as they move from one stage to another. In practical terms, visibility allows participants to answer questions such as: Where was the shipment last recorded? Who was responsible for it? Was it picked up? Was delivery confirmed? When organizations maintain separate databases, the answers may be delayed, incomplete, or inconsistent. A customer may see one status, a carrier may have another record, and a warehouse may rely on a separate document. Research has identified information sharing, traceability, and trust as continuing challenges in modern supply chain management [1].

Most traditional tracking systems are centralized. A company or platform controls the database, decides who can change it, and provides information to other participants. Centralized systems can be effective and efficient, but they require participants to trust the organization operating the system. They can also create a single point of control and, in some cases, a single point of failure. If information is entered incorrectly, updated late, or changed without a clear history, outside participants may have limited ways to independently verify what happened.

Blockchain is a distributed ledger technology that records transactions in linked blocks. Each confirmed block is connected to earlier records through cryptographic methods. This structure makes past entries difficult to change without detection. Bitcoin demonstrated how a network could maintain a shared transaction history without depending on one central financial institution [2]. Later blockchain platforms, including Ethereum, expanded this idea by supporting smart contracts. A smart contract is a program stored and executed on a blockchain. It can enforce predefined rules and produce a visible transaction history when its functions are used.

These features support supply chain tracking because several participants can refer to the same record instead of depending entirely on separate copies. Blockchain-based systems have been proposed for product provenance, food traceability, logistics, finance, and Internet of Things applications [3], [4], [5], [7], [8]. The literature commonly identifies transparency, traceability, data integrity, and automation as possible benefits. At the same time, researchers warn that blockchain adoption involves tradeoffs, including transaction cost, performance, privacy, interoperability, governance, and the difficulty of connecting physical events to digital records [1], [4], [6], [7], [8].

The connection between physical goods and blockchain data is especially important. A blockchain can preserve the information it receives, but it cannot independently determine whether a container is at a stated location or whether a photograph is genuine. People, scanners, GPS devices, or other trusted data sources must provide that information. This is sometimes described as the gap between on-chain records and off-chain events. Therefore, blockchain strengthens the record of reported events; it does not replace tracking equipment, organizational procedures, or human judgment [1].

## 1.2 Motivation for the Study

The idea for this research developed from both my professional experience and a later conversation with a former university classmate. I have approximately fifteen years of professional supply chain and logistics experience in the United States Navy. I began my military service as an enlisted Logistics Specialist and later commissioned as a Naval Supply Officer. During a period of fifteen years, I have seen firsthand how difficult it is to maintain visibility and accountability over supplies moving through a large, complex, and globally distributed logistics network. For many years, I assumed that misplaced, delayed, or difficult to locate material was largely due to the large size and complexity of the Navy’s supply chain, which spans multiple organizations, transportation systems, and geographic regions.

That assumption changed after a conversation with a former university classmate who worked for a logistics company in Guam. She described an experience in which that company had difficulty keeping track of shipping containers and supplies. Guam has a land area of approximately 210 square miles, so the experience was surprising. I had expected supply visibility problems to be more closely associated with very large and geographically dispersed logistics networks, yet here was a much smaller operation in a limited geographic area facing a similar challenge. This made me realize that supply chain visibility problems are not necessarily limited to the size of an organization or the distance over which goods travel. Even smaller logistics environments can struggle when records are fragmented across people, documents, systems, and handoffs. This raised a practical question: if shipment visibility can be difficult in both large global supply networks and smaller geographically limited operations, what kind of information system could provide a clearer and more verifiable history of product movement?

This experience provided the motivation for the study. The example helped identify a broader technical problem: shipment records may be divided among people, documents, and software systems, making it difficult to reconstruct a complete history when something goes wrong. The research question grew from that observation, but the thesis evaluates a technical prototype rather than making general claims about Guam's logistics industry.

The project later developed into the Reliable Logistics Exchange, a prototype focused on delivery tracking. The acronym RLE was originally inspired by the initials of my children, Richard, Lucas and Eva. I later developed the name Reliable Logistics Exchange to reflect the system’s purpose of exchanging logistics information among participants. Therefore, the name has both a functional meaning within the project and a personal significance to me.

Throughout this thesis, RLE refers to the complete platform or system, while RLE token refers to the digital reward used within the platform. Although the original motivation involved shipping containers and supplies, the prototype uses a simplified delivery lifecycle that can be tested in a controlled environment. A customer creates a delivery, a driver is assigned, authorized users record checkpoints and proof hashes, and the delivery can be completed with a rating. This smaller workflow provides a practical way to study the main idea of shared and tamper-resistant event records without claiming that the prototype is already a complete commercial container-management system.

## 1.3 Research Gap

Existing research provides strong theoretical support for using blockchain in supply chains [4], [5], and a number of studies describe industry-specific systems or technical architectures [1], [6], [7], [8]. However, a gap remains between discussing blockchain's possible benefits and evaluating an integrated application that people can interact with. A conceptual framework may explain why immutability or distributed control could be useful, but it does not show how users create deliveries, connect wallets, submit tracking events, retrieve records, or respond to transaction and usability problems.

Another gap is how a blockchain system actually becomes decentralized after it has already been built and is being used. Previous research has looked at the challenge of balancing centralized leadership with decentralized control in blockchain supply chain systems. However, this is usually treated as an organizational problem instead of something that can be controlled directly through the system's code [6]. In real projects, teams may say that they plan to give up control and become more decentralized over time, but this does not always happen. Once a system has users and begins making money, the people running it may not have much reason to give up their control [9].

Even when a project officially gives governance power to token holders, the control can still remain in the hands of only a few people. For example, one study of the Compound and Uniswap protocols found that only ten voters held most of the voting power in each system [10]. This shows that simply transferring control does not always mean that the system is truly decentralized.

RLE tries to address this problem by using a milestone-based smart contract. As the network reaches certain milestones, control is gradually transferred from the original administrators to the people participating in the system. The rules for this transfer are written into the smart contract, which means the change does not depend on someone deciding later whether they want to give up control. However, this thesis does not assume that RLE will automatically avoid the same problems seen in other blockchain systems. It is still possible that control could become concentrated among a small number of participants. Whether RLE can actually distribute control among many participants is one of the questions this thesis examines.

There is also a need for careful evaluation that separates what a prototype demonstrates from what it does not prove. A working smart contract can demonstrate that delivery events can be recorded and queried. It does not automatically prove that the system is ready for production, more accurate than every centralized system, resistant to every attack, or affordable at commercial scale. Similarly, a token-reward formula can demonstrate programmable incentives, but claims about fairness or resistance to manipulation require results that support those conclusions.

This thesis addresses the gap by documenting the construction and evaluation of a complete proof-of-concept system. RLE combines a user-facing Flutter application, Ethereum smart contracts, wallet interaction, Firestore services, automated contract tests, and simulation experiments. The study evaluates whether the system can represent a delivery lifecycle and provide a verifiable event history. It also identifies weaknesses, failed tests, security concerns, and limitations that must be addressed before broader deployment. In this way, the contribution is not only the prototype itself but also an honest account of what was learned by building and testing it.

## 1.4 Problem Statement

Supply chain participants may depend on fragmented or centrally controlled records that do not provide every stakeholder with the same ability to verify shipment events. When a shipment is delayed, misplaced, or disputed, participants may have difficulty determining which record is correct and whether an earlier entry was changed. The problem is not simply the absence of data; it is the absence of a shared, trustworthy, and auditable history across organizational boundaries.

Blockchain technology addresses this problem by recording delivery events on a ledger that is shared and difficult to modify after confirmation. However, a new logistics network may not have enough participants or an established governance process to operate in a fully decentralized way at launch. It may first require centralized administration for development, onboarding, and early decisions. Therefore, this research designs and evaluates RLE to examine how a blockchain-based logistics platform can provide transparent and auditable delivery tracking while creating a path from initial administrative control toward full decentralization.

## 1.5 Purpose and Central Thesis

The purpose of this study is to design, build, and test a blockchain-based system for tracking deliveries. The study looks at how Ethereum smart contracts can be used to record important delivery events, how a Flutter application can allow users to interact with the system, and how the platform could slowly move from centralized control to full decentralization.

This thesis argues that blockchain can be useful in logistics when different people or organizations need to share delivery information but do not fully trust one company to control all of the records. A blockchain logistics system does not have to be fully decentralized from the beginning. It can first be managed by a central group while the system is being developed and new users are joining. Over time, smart contracts and token rewards can be used to give full control to the people who participate in the network. RLE is used in this study to show how this type of transition could work and to examine both the benefits and the problems that may come with it.

This study does not claim that RLE can solve every problem in logistics. RLE is only a proof of concept and is not a complete system that is ready to be used by real companies. Blockchain also cannot guarantee that the information entered into the system is true, prevent packages from being lost, or replace tools such as GPS and barcode scanners. Instead, this study looks at whether blockchain can create a more reliable record after information is entered and what is needed to connect that record to an application that people can use.

## 1.6 Research Objectives

The primary objective is to develop and evaluate RLE as a proof of concept for transparent, auditable delivery tracking and a code-enforced transition from centralized administration to full decentralization.

The specific objectives are:

1. Design a delivery lifecycle that identifies the customer, assigned driver, delivery status, distance, value, timestamps, and completion information.
2. Develop Ethereum smart contracts that record delivery creation, hashed location checkpoints, pickup and delivery proof hashes, ratings, fees, and reward information.
3. Develop a Flutter application that allows users to connect a wallet and interact with the delivery system through a graphical interface.
4. Evaluate the prototype through automated smart-contract tests, integration scenarios, gas measurements, and controlled simulations.
5. Examine whether the recorded events provide a transparent and auditable delivery history while protecting exact location information through hashing.
6. Identify technical, security, privacy, usability, governance, and scalability limitations that would affect future development or real-world deployment.
7. Evaluate whether the milestone-based smart contract and token-reward system can support a genuine transition to full decentralization, without assuming that their fairness or effectiveness has already been proven.

## 1.7 Research Questions

The study is guided by the following research questions:

- **RQ1:** How can a blockchain-based delivery system support transparent and auditable shipment records among independent participants?
- **RQ2:** How can Ethereum smart contracts and a user-facing application be integrated to support a delivery lifecycle from creation through completion?
- **RQ3:** How can RLE begin under centralized administration and use smart contracts, participant roles, token-based participation, and decentralization milestones to support a gradual transfer to full decentralization?
- **RQ4:** What functional, security, performance, privacy, usability, and governance limitations appear when RLE is evaluated?

These questions place shared records, implementation, and progressive decentralization at the center of the thesis. They also require a balanced evaluation of RLE's limitations, including whether its milestone-based transition results in meaningfully distributed control. Conclusions about token rewards and decentralization must be limited to what the prototype and measured results can support.

## 1.8 Scope and Delimitations

The scope of this research is limited to a proof-of-concept delivery application. The blockchain portion uses Solidity smart contracts and the Ethereum development environment provided by Hardhat. The application uses Flutter, wallet services, and Firebase/Firestore support. The prototype is designed mainly for a local research network rather than a public production blockchain. It represents customer, driver, and administrator roles and supports a simplified delivery process.

RLE records hashes of location and proof information rather than storing full photographs or readable GPS coordinates directly on the blockchain. This design demonstrates how a blockchain can verify that a particular piece of information was submitted while limiting the amount of private or costly data placed on-chain. Complete files and real-time sensor information would require secure off-chain storage and a trusted method for connecting those files to their hashes.

The evaluation focuses on software behavior in a controlled environment. It includes contract and integration tests, selected gas measurements, and simulations of reward behavior. It does not include a commercial deployment, a large group of human participants, a comparison with proprietary company databases, or field testing with physical shipping containers. As a result, the findings show technical feasibility and reveal design limitations, but they should not be generalized as proof of performance across all supply chains.

The reward system is also exploratory. It calculates RLE token rewards using delivery volume, distance, rating, and delivery diversity, with limits intended to reduce simple forms of manipulation. The decentralization contract tracks milestones for moving from founder control toward shared governance. These components demonstrate how incentives and governance rules can be programmed, but their economic fairness and resistance to coordinated attacks require further refinement and broader testing. In particular, this study does not include a large enough number of independent participants to determine whether control becomes meaningfully distributed once the milestones are reached, or whether it instead concentrates among a small number of participants, as has been observed in other blockchain governance systems [10].

## 1.9 Significance of the Study

This study has practical significance because it demonstrates a complete path from a supply chain visibility problem to a functioning software prototype. A reader can examine how a delivery is represented, how an authorized participant records a checkpoint, how proof information is linked through a hash, and how the resulting event can be queried. The system therefore provides a concrete example of blockchain-based tracking rather than only a theoretical description.

Academically, the study treats progressive decentralization itself as a design problem. Unlike prior work, which generally treats the shift toward decentralization as an organizational or voluntary decision, RLE encodes the transition directly into a smart contract, so that the change in control does not depend on a future decision by the system's administrators. RLE explores how a platform can begin with centralized administration and include mechanisms intended to distribute full control to participants as participation grows. Successful tests show which parts of the delivery lifecycle and transition model can be represented with smart contracts. Failed tests and simulation results identify areas where the implementation or research claims require correction. Reporting these limitations prevents the prototype from being presented as more mature than the evidence supports and provides a clearer foundation for future research.

Finally, the project also lays groundwork for future work involving secure device input, stronger access control, improved privacy, reproducible simulations, user studies, public or permissioned blockchain deployment, and integration with existing logistics systems. The experience that motivated the study concerned one company, but the technical questions about shared records, verification, and accountability are relevant to many settings in which goods pass between participants who do not maintain one common database.

## 1.10 Organization of the Thesis

The remainder of this thesis is organized into four additional chapters.

Chapter 2 reviews the literature and technical concepts related to supply chain visibility, blockchain, smart contracts, decentralized applications, token-based participation, governance, and progressive decentralization. It also examines related blockchain-based supply chain systems and further explains the research gap addressed by RLE.

Chapter 3 explains the research method and presents the design and implementation of RLE. It describes the system requirements, architecture, delivery workflow, participant roles, Ethereum smart contracts, Flutter application, wallet integration, supporting Firebase services, token-reward system, and progressive-decentralization mechanisms. The chapter also explains the testing and evaluation procedures used in the study.

Chapter 4 presents and discusses the results of the prototype evaluation. It reports the smart-contract tests, integration scenarios, gas measurements, token simulations, and decentralization-related findings. The results are discussed in relation to the research questions, including the benefits and limitations of RLE and the conditions under which blockchain may be useful. The chapter also discusses security, privacy, usability, governance, and threats to validity.

Chapter 5 concludes the thesis by summarizing the major findings and explaining what the RLE prototype demonstrates. It also identifies the study's limitations and provides recommendations for future development, broader testing, and additional research.
